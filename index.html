A little reasoning goes a long way.

Track possibilities, not just coins.

“Coin 7 is heavy” and “coin 7 is light” are two different possibilities. Twelve coins start with 24 possible answers. Each weighing sorts the answers into three groups: left heavy, right heavy, or balanced.

A useful weighing leaves you with a manageable group whichever result you get. Four coins against four is a good first move for twelve coins: each outcome leaves eight possible answers.

The table has rules.

Exactly one coin is counterfeit. All genuine coins weigh the same. Each balance needs the same number of coins on both pans. An empty balance is allowed, but at least one must be in use. Coins can move freely between rounds.

You get one final answer. A correct answer within the round limit wins. You can still submit after the last weighing; there is no extra weighing after the limit.

More balances, one commitment.

Two balances do not give nine usable outcomes. Only one coin is fake, so only one balance can tilt. There are five outcomes: either balance tilts in either direction, or both balance. In general, b balances give at most 2b + 1 outcomes per round.

All pans are committed before any result appears. Sequential weighings let you react after each result; simultaneous balances let you react only after the whole round.

What does “minimum” mean?

It is the fewest rounds a strategy needs to guarantee success, for any fake coin and either direction. A lucky guess can finish sooner.

With no spare genuine coin, the capacity in w rounds on b balances is ((2b + 1)w − 1)/2 − b. We choose the smallest round count that covers your coins. This gives 12 coins in three weighings, or 10 coins in two rounds on two balances.

Thirteen coins need four weighings if you must also identify heavy or light. The extra possibility matters.

Source: Halbeisen & Hungerbühler, The general counterfeit coin problem (1995), Theorems 1–2. Playable range: 3–120 coins and 1–6 balances.
